The Living World: Ecosystems

Ecosystems, energy flow, biogeochemical cycles, biomes

~6–8%

Ecosystem Basics

  • Ecosystem — community of organisms + their abiotic environment
  • Biotic factors — living (plants, animals, bacteria)
  • Abiotic factors — nonliving (temp, water, light, soil)
  • Producer (autotroph) — makes own food via photosynthesis
  • Consumer (heterotroph) — eats other organisms
  • Decomposer — breaks down dead matter, returns nutrients to soil
  • Detritivore — eats dead organic matter (earthworms, dung beetles)

Energy Flow

  • 10% rule — only ~10% of energy transfers between trophic levels
  • Food chain — linear energy pathway
  • Food web — complex interconnected food chains
  • Trophic levels — producers → primary consumers → secondary → tertiary
  • Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) — total photosynthesis
  • Net Primary Productivity (NPP) — GPP minus plant respiration; energy available to consumers
  • Energy is lost as heat at each level — matter cycles, energy flows

Carbon Cycle

  • Carbon moves through atmosphere, organisms, oceans, and rock
  • Photosynthesis — pulls CO₂ from atmosphere into biomass
  • Respiration — releases CO₂ back to atmosphere
  • Combustion — burning fossil fuels releases stored carbon
  • Decomposition — releases CO₂ and methane
  • Carbon sinks — oceans, forests, soil absorb more carbon than they release
  • Ocean acidification — CO₂ dissolves in seawater → carbonic acid → lower pH

Nitrogen Cycle

  • N₂ gas makes up ~78% of atmosphere but most organisms can't use it directly
  • Nitrogen fixation — bacteria (Rhizobium) convert N₂ → ammonia (NH₃)
  • Nitrification — bacteria convert NH₃ → nitrites → nitrates (usable by plants)
  • Denitrification — bacteria convert nitrates back to N₂ gas
  • Assimilation — plants absorb nitrates; animals eat plants
  • Excess fertilizer → nutrient runoff → eutrophication

Phosphorus & Water Cycles

  • Phosphorus cycle — no atmospheric phase; moves through rock, soil, water; released by weathering
  • Phosphorus often the limiting nutrient in aquatic ecosystems
  • Water cycle — evaporation → condensation → precipitation → runoff/infiltration
  • Transpiration — water loss through plant leaves
  • Evapotranspiration — evaporation + transpiration combined
  • Groundwater — stored in aquifers; recharged slowly

Biomes

  • Tropical rainforest — high biodiversity, hot & wet year-round, thin nutrient-poor soil
  • Temperate deciduous forest — 4 seasons, moderate rainfall, fertile soil
  • Grassland/savanna — moderate rainfall, fires maintain structure
  • Desert — very low precipitation (<25cm/yr), extreme temps
  • Tundra — permafrost, very cold, low biodiversity, treeless
  • Taiga (boreal forest) — coniferous trees, cold, largest land biome
  • Chaparral — Mediterranean climate, fire-adapted shrubs

Ecological Succession

  • Primary succession — bare rock/new land (no soil); starts with pioneer species (lichens)
  • Secondary succession — disturbed land with soil remaining; faster
  • Pioneer species — first colonizers; tolerant of harsh conditions
  • Climax community — stable endpoint of succession
  • Each stage modifies the environment for the next

The Living World: Biodiversity

Natural selection, species interactions, ecosystem services

~6–8%

Natural Selection & Evolution

  • Individuals with traits better suited to environment survive and reproduce more
  • Adaptations — heritable traits that increase fitness
  • Speciation — formation of new species (often from geographic isolation)
  • Genetic diversity — variety within a species; buffer against disease/change
  • Bottleneck effect — population crash reduces genetic diversity

Species Interactions

  • Predation — predator benefits (+), prey harmed (−)
  • Competition — both harmed (−/−); for same resources
  • Mutualism — both benefit (+/+); e.g., bee-flower, mycorrhizae
  • Commensalism — one benefits, other unaffected (+/0); e.g., barnacles on whales
  • Parasitism — parasite benefits (+), host harmed (−); e.g., tapeworm
  • Keystone species — disproportionately large effect on ecosystem (e.g., sea otters)

Biodiversity & Ecosystem Services

  • Biodiversity — variety of life (genetic, species, ecosystem)
  • Biodiversity is highest in tropical regions near equator
  • Ecosystem services — provisioning (food, water), regulating (climate, floods), cultural, supporting (nutrient cycling)
  • Island biogeography — larger, less isolated islands have more species
  • Indicator species — sensitive to environmental change; signal ecosystem health (e.g., amphibians)

Threats to Biodiversity (HIPPO)

  • Habitat destruction — #1 cause of species loss
  • Invasive species — outcompete natives, no natural predators
  • Pollution — degrades habitat quality
  • Population growth (human) — demands more resources
  • Overharvesting — hunting/fishing beyond sustainable levels
  • Also: climate change, disease

Populations

Population dynamics, carrying capacity, human population

~10–15%

Population Growth Models

  • Exponential growth (J-curve) — unlimited resources; population grows faster as it gets larger
  • Logistic growth (S-curve) — growth slows as population approaches carrying capacity (K)
  • Carrying capacity (K) — maximum population an environment can sustain
  • Limiting factors — food, water, space, predation, disease slow growth
  • Density-dependent factors — effect increases as population grows (disease, competition)
  • Density-independent factors — affect population regardless of size (flood, fire)

Reproductive Strategies

  • r-selected species — many small offspring, little parental care, short lifespan (mice, insects, weeds)
  • K-selected species — few large offspring, lots of parental care, long lifespan (elephants, humans, whales)
  • Survivorship curves:
    — Type I: most survive to old age (K-selected, e.g., humans)
    — Type II: constant death rate (birds)
    — Type III: most die young (r-selected, e.g., oysters)

Human Population

  • World population ~8 billion; rapid growth since industrial revolution
  • Demographic transition — 4 stages from high birth/death rates to low both (as country develops)
  • Total Fertility Rate (TFR) — average children per woman; replacement level = 2.1
  • Age structure diagrams — wide base = growing population; narrow base = declining
  • Doubling time = 70 ÷ growth rate (%)
  • MDCs (developed) — low growth; LDCs (developing) — higher growth
Calc tip: Doubling time = 70 / r%. If growth rate is 2%, doubling time = 35 years.

Ecological Footprint & Sustainability

  • Ecological footprint — land/water needed to sustain a person's consumption
  • Biocapacity — amount of ecological services available
  • Overshoot — demand exceeds biocapacity
  • Developed nations have much larger per capita footprints
  • Sustainability — meeting present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs

Earth's Systems and Resources

Plate tectonics, soil, atmosphere layers, rock cycle

~10–15%

Plate Tectonics

  • Convergent boundary — plates collide; subduction, mountains, volcanoes, earthquakes
  • Divergent boundary — plates separate; new crust forms (mid-ocean ridges)
  • Transform boundary — plates slide past each other; earthquakes (e.g., San Andreas)
  • Subduction — denser oceanic plate sinks under continental plate
  • Earthquakes & volcanoes concentrated at plate boundaries ("Ring of Fire")

Soil

  • Soil horizons: O (organic litter) → A (topsoil, humus) → B (subsoil, leached minerals) → C (parent material) → R (bedrock)
  • Humus — decomposed organic matter; improves soil fertility and structure
  • Soil texture — sand (large, drains fast), silt (medium), clay (small, holds water/nutrients)
  • Loam — ideal mix of sand, silt, clay for agriculture
  • Soil erosion — removal of topsoil by wind/water; worsened by deforestation
  • Desertification — fertile land becomes desert; caused by overgrazing, deforestation, drought

Rock Cycle

  • Igneous rock — formed from cooled magma/lava (granite, basalt)
  • Sedimentary rock — formed from compressed sediment layers (sandstone, limestone); contains fossils
  • Metamorphic rock — existing rock transformed by heat/pressure (marble, slate)
  • Weathering — physical or chemical breakdown of rock into sediment
  • Fossil fuels form from organic matter compressed in sedimentary rock over millions of years

Atmosphere Layers

  • Troposphere — 0–12km; weather occurs here; temp decreases with altitude
  • Stratosphere — 12–50km; contains ozone layer; temp increases with altitude (ozone absorbs UV)
  • Mesosphere — 50–80km; coldest layer; meteors burn up here
  • Thermosphere — 80–700km; very hot; auroras occur here
  • Coriolis effect — Earth's rotation deflects wind/water; right in NH, left in SH

Solar Radiation & Earth's Energy

  • Albedo — reflectivity of a surface; ice/snow high albedo; ocean/forest low albedo
  • Tropics receive most direct solar energy; poles receive least
  • Seasons — caused by Earth's axial tilt (23.5°), not distance from sun
  • El Niño — weakened trade winds → warm water moves east; drought in Australia/SE Asia, rain in W. Americas
  • La Niña — opposite of El Niño; stronger trade winds

Wind & Ocean Patterns

  • Trade winds — blow from east in tropics (Hadley cells)
  • Jet streams — fast-moving air in upper troposphere; affect weather
  • Upwelling — cold, nutrient-rich water rises from deep ocean; highly productive (e.g., Peru)
  • Thermohaline circulation — "ocean conveyor belt"; driven by temp and salinity differences; distributes heat globally

Land and Food

Agriculture, land use, food production impacts

~15–20%

Agriculture Basics

  • Green Revolution — 1960s–70s; high-yield crops, synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, irrigation → huge food output increase
  • Monoculture — single crop grown over large area; efficient but vulnerable to pests/disease
  • Crop rotation — alternating crops to restore soil nutrients
  • Cover crops — grown off-season to prevent erosion, fix nitrogen
  • GMOs — genetically modified organisms; higher yield, pest resistance; controversial
  • Organic farming — no synthetic inputs; lower yield but less environmental impact

Soil Degradation & Irrigation

  • Salinization — salt buildup in irrigated soil; makes land infertile
  • Waterlogging — over-irrigation saturates soil; deprives roots of oxygen
  • Contour plowing — plowing along hillside contours to reduce erosion
  • Terracing — cutting flat steps into hillsides for farming
  • Windbreaks (shelterbelts) — rows of trees to slow wind erosion
  • No-till farming — avoids plowing; reduces erosion, preserves soil structure

Pesticides & Fertilizers

  • Pesticides — insecticides, herbicides, fungicides; kill target & non-target species
  • Biological magnification (bioaccumulation) — toxins concentrate up the food chain (DDT → osprey eggshells)
  • Integrated Pest Management (IPM) — minimize pesticide use; use biological controls, crop rotation, resistant varieties
  • Eutrophication — excess nitrogen/phosphorus fertilizer runoff → algal blooms → oxygen depletion → dead zones
  • Hypoxic zones: Gulf of Mexico dead zone (from Mississippi River ag runoff)

Meat Production & Food Systems

  • Producing 1 calorie of beef requires ~10+ calories of grain
  • CAFOs — concentrated animal feeding operations; efficient but generate pollution (manure runoff, methane)
  • Aquaculture — fish farming; reduces pressure on wild fisheries but can pollute
  • Overfishing — harvesting fish faster than they can reproduce; collapse of fisheries
  • Bycatch — unintended species caught in fishing nets

Land Use & Deforestation

  • Deforestation — increases CO₂, reduces biodiversity, increases erosion, disrupts water cycle
  • Tropical rainforests are most at risk; cleared for agriculture and cattle
  • Slash and burn — cut + burn forest for farmland; nutrients rapidly depleted
  • Urban sprawl — low-density expansion of cities into surrounding land; increases impervious surfaces → more runoff
  • Habitat fragmentation — splitting habitat into patches; isolates populations

Mining & Resource Extraction

  • Surface mining — removes overlying material (strip mining, open-pit); cheaper but highly disruptive
  • Subsurface mining — underground tunnels; less surface damage but more dangerous
  • Acid mine drainage — sulfide minerals + water + oxygen → sulfuric acid → toxic runoff
  • Mountaintop removal — blasts off mountaintops for coal; buries streams
  • Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act (SMCRA) — requires land restoration after mining

Energy Resources and Consumption

Fossil fuels, nuclear, renewables, energy policy

~15–20%

Fossil Fuels

  • Formed over millions of years from compressed organic matter — nonrenewable
  • Coal — solid; burning releases most CO₂; produces SO₂ (acid rain)
  • Petroleum — liquid; refined into gasoline, diesel, plastics
  • Natural gas — mostly methane (CH₄); cleanest fossil fuel but still emits CO₂
  • Fracking (hydraulic fracturing) — injects fluid to fracture rock and release gas; water contamination risk
  • EROI — Energy Return on Investment; ratio of energy gained to energy spent to get it

Nuclear Energy

  • Nuclear fission — splitting heavy atoms (uranium-235) releases massive energy
  • No greenhouse gas emissions during operation
  • Cooling towers — release steam (water vapor), not smoke
  • Radioactive waste — remains dangerous for thousands of years; no permanent storage solution in US
  • Chernobyl (1986), Fukushima (2011) — major accident examples; long-term contamination
  • Nuclear fusion — combining light atoms; cleaner, but not yet commercially viable

Renewable Energy

  • Solar PV — photovoltaic cells convert sunlight to electricity
  • Concentrated solar — mirrors focus sunlight to heat fluid and drive turbines
  • Wind turbines — convert kinetic energy of wind to electricity
  • Hydropower — dams use falling water to spin turbines; most-used renewable globally
  • Tidal/wave energy — uses ocean movement; limited locations
  • Geothermal — uses Earth's heat; Iceland, California examples

Biofuels & Energy Efficiency

  • Ethanol — from corn/sugarcane; blended with gasoline; corn ethanol has poor EROI
  • Biodiesel — from vegetable oils/animal fats; lower emissions than diesel
  • Biomass — burning wood, waste, crops; technically renewable but emits CO₂
  • Energy efficiency — more work per unit energy; saves money + reduces emissions
  • Cogeneration (CHP) — captures waste heat from electricity generation for heating
  • Green building / LEED — standards for energy-efficient construction

Atmospheric Pollution

Air pollutants, acid rain, ozone depletion, indoor pollution

~7–10%

Primary vs. Secondary Pollutants

  • Primary pollutant — emitted directly (CO, SO₂, NOₓ, particulates, lead)
  • Secondary pollutant — formed in atmosphere through chemical reactions (ozone O₃, smog, sulfuric acid)
  • Smog — photochemical smog (NOₓ + VOCs + sunlight → ozone); industrial smog (SO₂ + particulates)
  • Thermal inversion — warm air traps cool polluted air near ground; traps smog

Key Air Pollutants

  • CO (carbon monoxide) — from incomplete combustion; binds hemoglobin; odorless, deadly
  • SO₂ (sulfur dioxide) — from burning coal; → acid rain; respiratory irritant
  • NOₓ (nitrogen oxides) — from cars/power plants; → smog + acid rain
  • Particulate matter (PM) — fine particles from combustion; lung disease
  • VOCs (volatile organic compounds) — from solvents, cars; form smog
  • Lead — neurotoxin; eliminated from gasoline (Clean Air Act)

Acid Rain

  • SO₂ + NOₓ react with water vapor → sulfuric acid + nitric acid → pH drops in rain
  • Normal rainwater pH ~5.6; acid rain pH <5.6
  • Damages forests, acidifies lakes (kills fish), weathers stone buildings
  • Scrubbers — remove SO₂ from power plant emissions before release
  • Catalytic converters — reduce NOₓ and CO from vehicles
  • Can be a transboundary problem — pollutants travel far from source

Stratospheric Ozone Depletion

  • Ozone (O₃) in stratosphere absorbs harmful UV-B radiation
  • CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) — from refrigerants, aerosols; break down ozone in stratosphere
  • Cl from CFCs catalytically destroys ozone molecules
  • Ozone hole — largest over Antarctica in spring; UV increase → skin cancer, cataracts
  • Montreal Protocol (1987) — international treaty to phase out CFCs; ozone layer is recovering
  • Note: ground-level ozone (troposphere) is a pollutant; stratospheric ozone is protective

Indoor Air Pollution

  • Radon — naturally occurring radioactive gas from uranium decay in soil; seeps into basements; #1 cause of lung cancer after smoking
  • Asbestos — insulation material; fibers cause mesothelioma
  • Lead paint — in older homes; neurotoxin especially for children
  • Formaldehyde — from building materials, furniture; carcinogen
  • CO — from faulty heaters/stoves; install CO detectors
  • Cooking fires in developing countries — major cause of indoor air pollution deaths globally

Clean Air Act & Regulations

  • Clean Air Act (1970, amended 1990) — EPA sets National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for 6 "criteria pollutants"
  • 6 criteria pollutants: CO, SO₂, NOₓ, O₃, PM, Pb
  • Cap and trade — set pollution limit; companies buy/sell allowances
  • Best Available Control Technology (BACT) — required for new sources

Aquatic and Terrestrial Pollution

Water pollution, solid waste, toxic pollutants

~7–10%

Water Pollution Types

  • Point source — identifiable single source (pipe from factory, sewage outflow)
  • Nonpoint source — diffuse, hard to trace (agricultural runoff, urban runoff)
  • Eutrophication — nutrient enrichment → algal bloom → oxygen depletion → hypoxia → dead zone
  • Thermal pollution — warm water discharged from power plants; decreases dissolved oxygen
  • Sediment pollution — eroded soil clouds water, smothers organisms

Waterborne Pathogens & Treatment

  • Untreated sewage carries cholera, typhoid, E. coli, hepatitis
  • BOD (Biological Oxygen Demand) — amount of O₂ needed to decompose organic waste; high BOD = polluted water
  • Water treatment: screening → settling → filtration → chlorination
  • Septic systems — on-site wastewater treatment; can leach into groundwater
  • Clean Water Act (1972) — regulates discharge of pollutants into US waterways; "fishable and swimmable"

Groundwater & Drinking Water

  • Aquifer — underground rock layer saturated with water
  • Ogallala Aquifer — largest US aquifer; being depleted faster than recharged
  • Saltwater intrusion — over-pumping coastal aquifers draws in seawater
  • Safe Drinking Water Act (1974) — EPA sets maximum contaminant levels (MCLs)
  • Flint, Michigan — example of lead water contamination from aging pipes

Solid Waste & Hazardous Waste

  • Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) — household/commercial trash
  • Landfills — lined to prevent leachate; produce methane (can be captured for energy)
  • Incineration — reduces volume, generates energy; releases toxins if not filtered
  • Recycling — reduces energy use, raw material extraction
  • Composting — organic matter → nutrient-rich soil amendment
  • Superfund (CERCLA) — EPA program to clean up hazardous waste sites; "polluter pays"

Toxic & Persistent Pollutants

  • Heavy metals — mercury, lead, arsenic; bioaccumulate; neurotoxins
  • Mercury — from coal burning; methylmercury in fish; Minamata disease
  • PCBs / DDT / dioxins — persistent organic pollutants (POPs); fat-soluble, bioaccumulate
  • Bioaccumulation — toxins build up in individual organisms
  • Biomagnification — toxin concentration increases at higher trophic levels
  • Endocrine disruptors — mimic hormones (BPA, atrazine); reproductive effects

Ocean Pollution

  • Plastic pollution — persists for centuries; microplastics ingested by marine life
  • Great Pacific Garbage Patch — vast accumulation of plastics in Pacific gyre
  • Oil spills — coat birds/mammals, smother bottom-dwellers (Deepwater Horizon, 2010)
  • Oil dispersants — break oil into droplets; toxic to marine life
  • Marine dead zones from agricultural runoff (Gulf of Mexico largest)

Global Change

Climate change, ozone, species loss, sustainability solutions

~15–20%

The Greenhouse Effect & Climate Change

Greenhouse Gases (GHGs)

  • CO₂ — burning fossil fuels, deforestation; most abundant GHG by volume
  • Methane (CH₄) — livestock, rice paddies, landfills, wetlands; 25× more potent than CO₂ over 100 yrs
  • N₂O — fertilizers, animal waste; ~265× more potent than CO₂
  • Water vapor — most abundant GHG; amplifies warming
  • CFCs — refrigerants; potent GHGs + ozone destroyers

Climate Change Impacts

  • Rising sea levels (thermal expansion + ice melt)
  • More intense storms, droughts, floods
  • Coral bleaching (thermal stress)
  • Shifting species ranges and migration patterns
  • Permafrost thaw → releases more methane (positive feedback)
  • Ocean acidification (CO₂ + H₂O → carbonic acid)
  • Arctic sea ice loss → less albedo → more warming (positive feedback)
Positive feedback loop example: Ice melts → lower albedo → more heat absorbed → more ice melts. This accelerates warming. A negative feedback dampens change (e.g., more evaporation → more clouds → less sunlight reaches surface).

Evidence for Climate Change

  • Rising atmospheric CO₂ (from 280 ppm pre-industrial → 420+ ppm today)
  • Rising average global temperatures (~1.1°C since pre-industrial)
  • Retreating glaciers and ice sheets worldwide
  • Rising sea levels (~20cm in past century)
  • Shifting seasons, earlier spring events
  • Ice core data — shows correlation of CO₂ and temperature over 800,000 years

Mitigation vs. Adaptation

  • Mitigation — reduce causes; cut GHG emissions (switch to renewables, efficiency, carbon capture)
  • Adaptation — adjust to effects; sea walls, drought-resistant crops, early warning systems
  • Carbon sequestration — capturing CO₂ (reforestation, soil, CCS technology)
  • Carbon tax — price per ton of CO₂ emitted; incentivizes reduction
  • Cap and trade — cap on total emissions; companies trade allowances

International Environmental Agreements

  • Montreal Protocol (1987) — phase out CFCs; most successful international environmental treaty
  • Kyoto Protocol (1997) — GHG reduction targets for developed nations; US never ratified
  • Paris Agreement (2015) — keep warming below 2°C; voluntary national pledges (NDCs)
  • CITES — regulates international trade of endangered species
  • UN Convention on Biological Diversity — protect biodiversity globally

Habitat Conservation

  • Endangered Species Act (ESA, 1973) — protects listed species and critical habitat in the US
  • Biosphere reserves — core protected + surrounding buffer zones
  • Wildlife corridors — connect habitat fragments; allow species movement
  • Restoration ecology — rehabilitate degraded ecosystems
  • Ex-situ conservation — zoos, seed banks, captive breeding
  • In-situ conservation — protect species in their natural habitat

Sustainability Solutions

  • Sustainable development — meeting current needs without compromising future generations
  • Renewable energy transition — solar, wind, hydro replace fossil fuels
  • Circular economy — reduce waste; resources loop back instead of landfill
  • Smart growth / New urbanism — denser, walkable cities; less sprawl
  • Ecotourism — sustainable tourism that funds conservation
  • Environmental justice — fair distribution of environmental benefits and burdens

Key US Environmental Laws

  • Clean Air Act (1970) — regulates air pollutants; created EPA
  • Clean Water Act (1972) — regulates water discharges
  • Safe Drinking Water Act (1974) — drinking water standards
  • CERCLA / Superfund (1980) — hazardous waste cleanup
  • NEPA (1969) — environmental impact statements required for federal projects
  • Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) — regulates solid and hazardous waste

FRQ Tips — How to Maximize Your Score

Structure & Format

  • • There are 3 FRQs — 70 minutes total (~23 min each)
  • • Answer each part separately and label them (a, b, c...)
  • • Define → Apply → Explain for each concept
  • • No bullet points needed — full sentences earn points
  • • Show your math work for calculation questions
  • • Units matter — always include them in calculations
  • • Don't contradict yourself — wrong follow-up cancels right answer

Most Common FRQ Topics

  • • Energy calculations (EROI, % efficiency, 10% rule)
  • • Population growth / doubling time math
  • • Eutrophication cause-and-effect chains
  • • Greenhouse effect + climate change impacts
  • • Comparing energy sources (pros/cons)
  • • Soil erosion and agricultural solutions
  • • Data/graph interpretation (always one per exam)
